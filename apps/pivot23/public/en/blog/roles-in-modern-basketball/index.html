Roles in modern basketball: from point guard to positionless player

PIVOT 23 team · 3 min read

For decades basketball was described through five well-defined positions: point guard, shooting guard, small forward, power forward and center. Each had specific duties and a typical body type. That division still exists today, but it has become much more fluid. In this article we look at how roles have evolved, why there's so much talk about "positionless" basketball, and what it means for anyone building a player, on the court or in a game.

The five classic positions

The point guard was the playmaker: he brought the ball up, called the plays and ran the offense. He was usually the shortest and quickest player.

The shooting guard was the main outside shooting threat and scored coming off screens.

The small forward was the most versatile player: big enough to guard wings, agile enough to attack off the dribble.

The power forward worked near the basket, grabbed rebounds and set screens, often with a good mid-range shot.

The center was the anchor under the basket: rim protection, rebounding and back-to-the-basket play.

What has changed

The main change is about space. As the three-point shot grew, teams realized that spreading the floor creates more room to drive and to pass. That changed what is asked of almost every position.

Centers, for example, now often need to pass from the high post, defend away from the basket and, in many cases, shoot from outside. Forwards need to guard several types of opponents. Point guards are no longer just playmakers: many are also their team's main scorers.

"Positionless" basketball

"Positionless" basketball describes teams where players have similar skills and can swap duties. On defense this allows switching every screen; on offense it lets anyone bring the ball up or shoot.

In reality positions haven't disappeared. They've become less about height and more about skills. Today people talk about a "primary creator", a "shooter", a "perimeter defender", a "rim protector" or a "passing big". These labels describe what a player does, not just where he stands.

Why versatility matters

A versatile player is valuable because he gives the coach options. He can play in different lineups, adapt to opponents and cover for missing teammates. That's why many young players today work on skills that once wouldn't have been expected of their position, like ball handling for a big man or post play for a guard.

That said, specialization still has value. A great shooter or an elite defender can have a long career without doing everything. The key is to have at least one high-level skill and no weaknesses that opponents can easily exploit.

A useful lens for fans

Understanding how roles have evolved helps you watch games differently. Instead of asking "who's the center?", you can look at who creates, who spaces the floor, who protects the rim and who adapts to switches. It's a richer way to read basketball.

In short

The five classic positions still exist as a reference, but modern basketball rewards versatility and describes players by what they can do. Having a clear strength and few weaknesses is still the recipe for a long career, on the hardwood as in a simulator.
