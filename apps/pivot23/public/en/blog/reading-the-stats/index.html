Stats

Reading the stats: beyond points per game

PIVOT 23 team · 3 min read

In basketball, numbers are everywhere. Box scores, season averages, scoring leaderboards. But the most famous numbers aren't always the most useful for understanding a player's value. In this article we look at how to read statistics more carefully, which questions to ask when you see a number, and why this skill also helps you play a simulator like PIVOT 23 better.

Points aren't everything

Points per game is the most quoted stat, and that makes sense: winning means scoring more than your opponent. But an isolated number can mislead. A player can score a lot because he takes a lot of shots, not necessarily because he makes them efficiently. Another can score less but help his team in a thousand ways the box score doesn't record.

So the first question to ask about a scoring average is: on how many shots? And in how many minutes?

Efficiency: scoring well, not just a lot

That's why efficiency stats were created. Shooting percentages are the starting point, but they aren't enough on their own, because a three is worth more than a two and free throws carry their own weight. There are metrics that account for all of this and let you compare players with different styles.

You don't need to memorize the formulas. Just remember the principle: for the same number of points, the player who uses fewer possessions helps his team more.

The pace of the game

Another often overlooked factor is pace. A team that plays fast has more possessions per game, and therefore more chances for everyone to pile up numbers. To compare players on different teams, or from different eras, analysts often use stats scaled to a fixed number of possessions or minutes.

This helps explain, for example, why numbers from eras with very different paces can't be compared directly.

Assists, turnovers and rebounds

Assists measure part of playmaking, but not all of it: a pass that leads to free throws, or one that breaks down the defense without becoming an assist, isn't counted. Turnovers should be read together with assists, because players who handle the ball a lot inevitably lose it more.

Rebounds also need context. A big man who boxes out his opponent can let a teammate grab the rebound: the credit is his, but the number goes to someone else.

Defense: the hardest to measure

Steals and blocks are the most visible defensive stats, but they tell only a small part of the story. A good defender often shows in what doesn't happen: shots not taken, passes not made, opponents forced into worse choices. That's why defensive evaluation remains one of the most debated topics among analysts.

Context first

Every number has to be read in context. Role on the team, teammates, the coach's system, minutes played and physical health all change what a number means. A player who moves from one team to another can see his numbers change without his actual value changing.

Stats in PIVOT 23

In PIVOT 23 every season produces stats that describe how the player is doing: points, rebounds, assists and other figures, plus end-of-season honors. Reading them critically helps you understand what's working in the career and where there's room to improve.

We don't reveal how the game generates these numbers, but the principles in this article apply there too: a season with fewer points isn't necessarily a worse season, and context matters.

Five questions to ask about a number

  1. In how many minutes or possessions was it achieved?
  2. With what efficiency?
  3. In what role and with which teammates?
  4. What isn't being measured?
  5. Is it a stable trend or a one-off season?

In short

Statistics are an extraordinary tool for understanding basketball, but they need to be read carefully. Efficiency, pace and context matter as much as totals. Learning to ask the right questions makes both watching games and playing a career more interesting.
