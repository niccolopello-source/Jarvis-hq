# Game design: why hard choices make a story memorable

What makes a simulation game truly engaging? Not the number of menus or stats, but the feeling that your decisions matter. In this article we share some game design ideas that guided the development of PIVOT 23 and that apply, more broadly, to anyone who loves games where you build a story.

## An interesting choice has a cost

One of the most quoted ideas in game design is that a game is a series of interesting choices. But when is a choice interesting? Usually when no option is clearly better than the others. If one road always wins, players find it quickly and the decision stops meaning anything.

That's why, in a career simulator, every crossroads should have a cost. Choosing visibility may mean giving something up financially; choosing security may mean passing on an opportunity. The player has to weigh upsides and risks, just as a real athlete would with his agent.

## Chance isn't the enemy

Many players are wary of randomness: if an outcome depends on luck, it feels unfair. Yet chance, used well, is what makes every run different and every story one of a kind.

The difference lies in how chance interacts with choices. If everything is random, decisions don't matter. If nothing is random, the game becomes a calculation. The balance lies in between: choices steer the career, but some surprises can catch you off guard, for better or worse. It's the same in real sport, where a perfect game or an injury can change everything.

## Help players understand without explaining everything

Another delicate balance is information. If a game hides too much, players feel lost. If it shows everything, including the internal rules, the experience turns into a spreadsheet and curiosity disappears.

In PIVOT 23 we chose to show what you need to decide, such as the player's profile, stats and season results, without revealing how the engine works. We want players to learn from experience, form hypotheses and test them in their next career. It's a form of discovery that makes every run more personal.

## Pacing matters as much as content

A career simulator covers many years in a short time. The risk is that everything flies by too fast, with no memorable moments, or too slowly, with too many screens to click through.

We worked to alternate quick moments, when seasons pass in a few taps, with pauses, when the game stops on an important decision or a result worth remembering. It's the same principle as film editing: big moments work better when there's space around them.

## Telling stories with data

Statistics are the natural language of basketball, and a simulator can't do without them. But numbers alone don't tell a story. That's why we try to present data in a way that brings out the key moments: the breakout season, the award year, the hard stretch after an injury.

The end-of-career summary is designed precisely as a story: a look back at what the player built, with all its highs and lows.

## Respect the player's time

A good game respects the time and attention of the people who play it. That means no unnecessary sign-ups, fast loading, reliable saves and an interface that stays clear even on a small screen. These choices are less visible than game mechanics, but they contribute a lot to the overall experience.

## Learn from players

Finally, design doesn't end at launch. Players' messages, their careers and their frustrations are a valuable source of ideas. Many changes come from there: a path that felt too easy, a moment that wasn't clear enough, a feature many people asked for.

## In short

- Choices are interesting when they have a cost.
- Chance makes stories unique, as long as it doesn't erase the value of decisions.
- Showing what's needed and leaving room for discovery keeps curiosity alive.
- Pacing, data presentation and respect for the player's time make the difference.

These are simple principles, but applying them well takes many iterations. It's the work we keep doing on PIVOT 23, one version at a time.
